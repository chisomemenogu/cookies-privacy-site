Cookies and Privacy Laws

Topic 2: Consent, regulation, and the future of tracking

Why Consent Matters

Many cookies are needed for a site to work, such as a cookie that keeps you logged in. Others track behavior for advertising. Privacy laws generally separate these two groups. In the European Union, the ePrivacy Directive requires consent before a site stores or reads information on a user's device, except for strictly necessary purposes. This is why many sites show a consent banner when you first arrive.

Note: A banner that is hard to decline, or that hides the reject option, can still break the rules. Regulators have fined companies for dark patterns of this kind.

GDPR and CCPA

Two major laws shape how cookies are used. The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) applies in the EU and the European Economic Area. The California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), as amended by the CPRA, applies to businesses that meet certain thresholds and do business with California residents.

GDPR compared with CCPA (summary)
Feature GDPR (EU) CCPA/CPRA (California)
Default rule for tracking Opt-in: consent is needed before non-essential cookies are set Opt-out: users can refuse the sale or sharing of their data
What counts as valid consent Freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous Clear opt-out mechanisms, such as a "Do Not Sell or Share" link
Penalties Up to 4% of global annual turnover or 20 million euros, whichever is higher Civil penalties enforced by the California Privacy Protection Agency and the Attorney General

Browsers and Third-Party Cookies

Browsers are also changing how cookies work. Safari and Firefox have blocked most third-party cookies by default for years. Google announced plans to phase out third-party cookies in Chrome, but it has changed that plan several times. Its Privacy Sandbox proposals aim to support advertising with less cross-site tracking. The result is an uneven landscape: the same website may behave differently depending on the browser a visitor uses.

Privacy Alternatives and Their Limits

Blocking cookies does not automatically stop tracking. Sites may turn to alternatives:

  • Local storage keeps data on the device without the automatic sending that cookies use.
  • Fingerprinting combines browser and device details to recognize a visitor without storing anything.
  • Logged-in identifiers link activity to an account across devices.

Privacy laws apply to many of these methods too. The EU rules on device access cover local storage, and the GDPR treats fingerprinting as personal data processing. Users can protect themselves with privacy settings, tracker blockers, and tools such as the Electronic Frontier Foundation's Cover Your Tracks test.
